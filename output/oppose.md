Ladies and gentlemen, I stand in opposition to the motion that strict laws must regulate Large Language Models—not because I believe they should run wild, but because strict regulation is the wrong tool, aimed at the wrong target, at the wrong time. It would cement today's fears into tomorrow's failures, and it would do so without making anyone safer.

First, strict regulation assumes that the greatest risk comes from the models themselves. It does not. The greatest risks come from how humans choose to use them. An LLM is a tool, like a printing press, a search engine, or a telephone. The printing press does not commit libel; the libeler does. The search engine does not spread propaganda; the propagandist does. To place primary legal liability on the developers of the tool is to abandon the very principle that makes law fair: personal responsibility. If I use a hammer to smash a window, we do not sue the hammer manufacturer for failing to make a "safe" hammer. We prosecute me. Strict laws aimed at LLM developers would create a fantasy where the machine is blamed, while the human who intentionally weaponized it walks free.

Second, strict regulation in this domain is impossible to write cleanly because LLMs are emergent, probabilistic, and context-dependent. There is no fixed "safe" version of a model that can be licensed like a drug or an aircraft. A model that passes every red-team test today can produce a harmful output tomorrow because language is infinite and users are creative. If we write strict laws that demand perfect safety, we simply outlaw the technology entirely or force it into the hands of the most powerful corporations—those with armies of lawyers to comply—while crushing small open-source developers, universities, and independent researchers. The result is not safety; it is concentrated power, which is precisely the opposite of what the motion's proponents claim to want.

Third, strict laws would freeze innovation at a dangerously premature stage. We are barely three years into the mass deployment of LLMs. We do not yet understand their full capabilities, their failure modes, or their long-term societal effects. Passing strict, rigid legislation now is like writing a constitution for a country that has not yet held its first census. Better to allow a period of calibrated, adaptive oversight—not strict bans and criminal penalties—during which we can observe, study, and update rules based on evidence. The motion treats every hallucination, every deepfake, and every biased output as a reason to lock down the entire field. But overregulation is itself a harm: it slows medical research, cripples legal aid for the poor, and prevents small teams from building accessible educational tools, all while sophisticated bad actors ignore the laws anyway because they operate outside the jurisdiction.

Fourth, strict laws create a false sense of security. A law that says "every LLM output must be traceable" sounds wonderful until you realize that bad actors will simply build unregulated models abroad, or use open-source weights, or encrypt their communications. The recent history of the internet proves this: we have strict laws against online fraud, child exploitation, and terrorist content, and yet these problems persist—because laws alone do not stop determined criminals. What strict regulation actually accomplishes is burdening law-abiding citizens and businesses with compliance costs while doing almost nothing to deter the bad actors who do not care about rules.

Finally, the motion's proponents invoke history: cars, airplanes, banks. But those technologies had one essential feature that LLMs do not—they were deterministic physical systems with clear, testable safety standards. A car has a brake distance. An airplane has an engine failure rate. An LLM has no analogous "safe threshold" because it is a statistical mirror of human language, including all of our biases, our sarcasm, our lies, and our creativity. We can regulate the use of LLMs in specific high-stakes domains—medical diagnosis, legal advice, automated hiring—through existing consumer protection and anti-discrimination laws, adapted sensibly. We do not need a new regime of strict, blanket control. We need targeted, flexible, evidence-based oversight, and we need it to evolve as the technology evolves.

In conclusion: strict laws to regulate LLMs are a sledgehammer for a scalpel problem. They will stifle the very innovation that could make these tools safer and more transparent over time. They will shift blame away from human actors and onto code that cannot be held morally accountable. And they will lull us into thinking that a piece of paper can control a technology that is only beginning to be understood. The motion asks us to act out of fear. I ask you to act out of wisdom: regulate the use, not the tool; hold the user accountable, not the silicon; and let us build the guardrails through careful iteration, not through a panic-stricken legal cage.

Thank you.
